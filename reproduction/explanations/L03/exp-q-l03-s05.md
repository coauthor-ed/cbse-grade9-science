# Two Halves, One Cell

## What each gamete is carrying

A human body cell holds 23 pairs - 46 chromosomes. Meiosis pulls the two members of each pair apart, and then separates each chromosome from its own copy, so every gamete it produces ends up with **23 chromosomes**: one from each pair.

That is the haploid number. A gamete is not short of chromosomes by accident; it is short of them on purpose.

## What happens when they meet

When two gametes fuse, their sets are **added together**. Nothing is halved and nothing is doubled.

- gamete one brings 23
- gamete two brings 23
- the fused cell holds 23 + 23 = **46**

46 is the full set - the same number as a body cell. That is what makes the new cell able to divide and develop at all.

## The step that is easy to get wrong

The tempting slip is to add the copies that were made during meiosis. They are not available.

Each chromosome is copied before the divisions so that both divisions have something to pull apart, and in the second division each chromosome is separated from its own identical copy - one copy to each new cell. So a gamete ends with one chromosome per pair, not one *pair*.

**A gamete cannot complete itself. It can only be completed by meeting another one.**

## Checking the answer

If you got 23, you have counted one gamete and stopped too early - that is what a single gamete carries, not what the new cell carries.

If you got 92, you have counted both the gametes and their copies. The copies were used up in meiosis and are not in the gamete at all.

46 is the only count that leaves a cell with one full set of instructions, half of it from each parent.
