# Five Bags, Two Varieties

## Sort the row before you spend anything

The row carries two kinds of flower, and they are not short of the same thing.

The first variety pollinates itself. Once a bud has been pollinated from its own stamens, it will make a pod whether or not anything else ever reaches it. **Bags on this variety protect a result that was already going to happen.**

The second variety cannot pollinate itself at all. Every flower on it is waiting for pollen to arrive. **Bags here do real work - they are the only thing standing between that flower and pollen from anywhere on the row.**

## One bud secures the easy variety

A bud, bagged, with its stamens left alone: the pollination happens inside the bud before the bag goes on, and the bag then keeps other pollen out. One bag, one secured pod.

Five bags on that variety would be five bags doing the same job once.

## Four flowers are worth spending on

The second variety's open flowers are where the remaining bags belong, and there is a second decision attached to each one: leave the stamens in.

A flower that has to receive pollen still has to have something to receive. Cut its stamens out and it has no pollen to give another plant, and no bud of its own to fall back on.

## The failed plans

- **Five bags on the variety that uses its own pollen.** One pod is secured and four bags are spent holding a result that was never at risk.
- **Cutting the stamens out and bagging.** This is the treatment the trial showed fails. Removing the stamens of a bud removes the only route pollen has to that flower, and the bag closes the other one.
- **Cutting the stamens out of the second variety's flowers.** This makes the row worse. Those flowers were already dependent on arriving pollen, and now they have nothing to contribute to the flowers that depend on them.

**Spend the bags where pollen is actually the missing thing.**
