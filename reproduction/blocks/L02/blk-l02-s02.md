## Spores from the air

The mould came from **spores** in the air, and the roti did not turn into it. Spores are so light that they float on air currents, and some settled on the roti before the lid closed.

<!-- STEP -->
## A moist chamber

Put a thin layer of cotton in a plastic box or steel dabba, cover it with tissue paper and moisten it with pre-boiled water. Lay the moist roti on top and keep it in a warm, dark place. Add a few drops of water if it starts to dry.

<!-- STEP -->
## Look every day

Spores need warmth (25-35 °C) and moisture. Move the slider through the days, and keep the lid on. After three days, use a magnifying glass to look at the mould, without touching it.

<!-- STEP -->
## Under the microscope

On a slide, mould stained with cotton blue shows long threads called **hyphae**. Round sacs at their tips are called sporangia, and each **sporangium** holds tiny round spores. One mould colony makes millions of spores, usually single-celled, and each can grow into a new individual.
