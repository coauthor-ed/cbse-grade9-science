# Oil, Water and a Little Soap

<!-- STEP -->
## Make an emulsion

Shake a few drops of cooking oil with water. For a moment you see tiny drops, and then the oil joins up and floats.

Now add a few drops of soap solution and shake again. This time the liquid stays cloudy, because the drops stay separate and spread through the water.

A colloid made of two liquids is called an **emulsion**. The soap is an **emulsifying agent**: it keeps the emulsion stable. Proteins do this job in milk and in butter.

<!-- STEP -->
## Two names for two parts

Every colloid has two parts. The solute-like part, here the tiny drops of oil, is the **dispersed phase**. The part it is spread through, here the water, is the **dispersion medium**.

Choose the drops, then the liquid around them, to see which is which.

<!-- STEP -->
## Oil in water, or water in oil

The same two liquids can be arranged in two ways:

- **Oil-in-water**: drops of oil in water. Milk and vanishing cream are like this.
- **Water-in-oil**: drops of water in oil. Butter, body lotion and cold cream are like this.

Some medicines are made as emulsions, which makes them easier to swallow and less greasy. To name an emulsion, ask which liquid forms the drops.

<!-- STEP -->
## Emulsions follow the size rule

The drops in an emulsion are of colloid size, so an emulsion scatters a beam, and its drops pass through filter paper. A strong spin can still separate some of them: a dairy spins milk in a machine to take off the cream.

**An emulsion is a colloid, so it behaves like one.**
