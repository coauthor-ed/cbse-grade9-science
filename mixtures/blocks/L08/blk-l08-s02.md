# The Size in the Middle

<!-- STEP -->
## Neither one nor the other

Both clues were real, so "solution" and "suspension" were reasonable answers, but neither is right. Your textbook calls blood a **colloid**, a third kind of mixture. Milk, tomato sauce and ice cream are colloids too.

These three kinds of mixture differ mainly in the size of their particles. Sizes this small are measured in nanometres (nm): one nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre.

<!-- STEP -->
## Three sizes

Move the dial from small to large and see which kind of mixture you get.

- **Less than 1 nm**: a solution, like salt in water.
- **1 to 1000 nm**: a colloid, like milk.
- **More than 1000 nm**: a suspension, like chalk in water.

**Solutions, colloids and suspensions differ mainly in particle size.**

<!-- STEP -->
## What the middle size does

Unlike a suspension, a colloid does not settle over time. Its particles stay spread evenly through the mixture, as they do in a solution. They are too small to see with your eyes, but they are there.

Plasma, the pale liquid from the top of a spun tube, is a colloid as well, because it carries proteins of this size.

**A note on blood.** Blood is an in-between case: its cells are large for a colloid, and in a still tube they slowly sink. Moving through the body, they stay spread out. Your textbook calls blood a colloid, so write that in your answers.

<!-- STEP -->
## Uniform to look at, but not homogeneous

You met these two words in the first lesson. A homogeneous mixture is uniform throughout, and no separate particles can be found in it. A heterogeneous mixture is not uniform: its parts can be seen, or they settle, or a beam of light shows them.

It is easy to call milk homogeneous, because it looks the same all through. But separate particles are there, too small for your eye to find.

**A colloid looks uniform, and it is still heterogeneous.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Why packet milk stays mixed

Leave fresh milk from a dairy for a few hours, and cream rises to the top. Its bigger drops of fat are less dense than water.

Packet milk is **homogenised**: it is forced through very narrow gaps, which breaks the fat into much smaller drops. These drops hardly rise at all, so no layer of cream forms. The name sounds like "homogeneous", but packet milk is still a colloid.
