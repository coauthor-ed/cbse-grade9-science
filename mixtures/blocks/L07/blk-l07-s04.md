# A Centrifuge Made of Cardboard

<!-- STEP -->
## The whole machine

A **paperfuge** is a cardboard disc with a loop of string through its centre and a handle at each end of the string. A very thin glass tube of blood is taped to the disc. It has no motor and it needs no electricity.

The idea comes from a common toy: a button spinning on a loop of thread. You may have made one.

<!-- STEP -->
## Pull the handles

Pull the handles apart and let them come back, again and again, in a steady rhythm. The string winds and unwinds, and the disc spins very fast.

Now watch the thin tube. The red blood cells are denser than the plasma, so they move outwards and pack at the far end. Pale plasma is left near the centre.

**This is the same separation as in the electric machine, done by hand.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Why two hands are enough

You might think that only a motor can spin fast enough. But the outward push depends on how fast the blood turns and how far it is from the centre. It does not depend on what does the turning.

A small, light disc on a string can turn very fast indeed. If you pull the handles slowly, the cells still move outwards, but they take much longer.

<!-- STEP -->
## Why it matters far from a hospital

Some clinics in remote areas have no steady electricity, so an electric centrifuge is no use there. A paperfuge is cheap, light and easy to carry.

It can help to detect two diseases. A short red layer can mean **anaemia**, which is too few red cells. For **malaria**, the spin gathers the infected cells in one place, so a health worker can find them more easily under a microscope.

**The method needs a difference in density and a fast spin. It does not need electricity.**
