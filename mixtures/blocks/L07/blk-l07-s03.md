# Three Layers From One Red Tube

<!-- STEP -->
## Before the spin

This tube holds blood with a small amount of a chemical added, which stops it from clotting. It is dark red, and it looks the same from top to bottom.

To keep the rotor balanced, a tube of water goes in on the opposite side. How many layers do you expect to see after one minute: one, two or three?

<!-- STEP -->
## After the spin

There are three layers. From the top:

- **Plasma**: a pale yellow liquid that fills a little over half the tube. It is the least dense part of blood.
- **Platelets and white blood cells**: a thin, pale layer in the middle.
- **Red blood cells**: a dark red layer at the bottom. They are the densest part, so they moved furthest out.

**The spin sorted the parts of blood by density.**

<!-- STEP -->
## The thin pale layer

Move the lens over the thin layer. White blood cells fight infection, and platelets help a cut to stop bleeding, so these two do very different jobs.

They end up together because both are denser than plasma and less dense than red blood cells. White blood cells are the largest cells here, but they do not go furthest out, because the spin depends on density and not on size.

<!-- STEP -->
## Why blood banks do this

Donated blood is tested and its blood group is found. It is then separated into plasma, platelets, and white and red blood cells. These are stored safely in blood banks until a patient needs them.

The body replaces donated blood naturally within a few weeks, which makes donating blood safe. Do you know your blood group?
