# Outward, Every Time

<!-- STEP -->
## The tube lies on its side

Each tube hangs in a small bucket on the arm of a part called the rotor. When the rotor starts to turn, the buckets swing out until the tubes lie almost **horizontal**, with their bottoms pointing away from the centre.

Now turn up the speed and watch the mud. It moves along the tube to the far end, and that far end is the bottom of the tube.

**In the machine, outward means towards the bottom of the tube.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Why not the centre?

The centre was a reasonable guess, because everything in the machine turns round it. But remember the spinning game: you felt pulled outwards, not inwards.

Anything moving in a circle feels an outward force, called the **centrifugal force**. It gets stronger as the turning gets faster. The mud particles are denser than the water around them, so they move outwards through it, and the water stays nearer the centre.

<!-- STEP -->
## Two layers

When the machine stops, the tube hangs upright again. Packed mud sits at the bottom, and clearer water sits on top.

These particles were sinking slowly in the jar as well, because mud is denser than water. The outward force in the machine is much stronger than the pull of gravity, so the same sinking takes minutes instead of days.

**A centrifuge uses a difference in density, and it makes that difference work faster.**

<!-- STEP -->
## What the speed changes

Run one tube at full speed and one at half speed. Both end with the same two layers. The slower tube takes about four times as long, because half the speed gives only a quarter of the push.

Laboratories separate the parts of blood in this way, and many chemical industries use large centrifuges too.

**A faster spin gives the same result in less time.**
