# Three Ways to Write a Concentration

<!-- STEP -->
## The answer is 9.1 %

Press the button and watch the bar. The salt slice stays 10 g long, but the whole bar grows from 100 g to 110 g.

"Still 10 %" is the answer most people give, because the salt did not change. But the percentage compares the salt with the **whole solution**, and the whole solution got bigger. 10 g out of 110 g is 9.1 %.

**The bottom of the fraction is always the solution, not the solvent.**

## Mass by mass: % m/m

This tells you how many grams of solute are in 100 g of solution. Your textbook's example is the jar you started with: 10 g of salt in 90 g of water.

Mass by mass percentage = (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 100

Mass of solution = 10 g + 90 g = 100 g.

So the answer is (10 g / 100 g) × 100 = **10 % m/m**.

It is used on packaged foods, and also for heterogeneous mixtures such as milk powder. In industry you will see it written as % w/w, which is the same number.

<!-- STEP -->
## Mass by volume: % m/v

This tells you how many grams of solute are in 100 mL of solution. It is used when measuring the volume of a liquid is easier than weighing it, as in medicines and laboratories.

Mass by volume percentage = (mass of solute / volume of solution) × 100

Dissolve 5 g of glucose in water and make 100 mL of solution. The answer is (5 g / 100 mL) × 100 = **5 % m/v**. The flask is filled up to 100 mL: you do not add 100 mL of water.

<!-- STEP -->
## Volume by volume: % v/v

This tells you how many millilitres of solute are in 100 mL of solution. It is used when two liquids are mixed, as in perfumes, cosmetics and vinegar.

Volume by volume percentage = (volume of solute / volume of solution) × 100

Mix 1 mL of liquid pesticide with enough water to make 100 mL of spray. The answer is (1 mL / 100 mL) × 100 = **1 % v/v**.

**All three ask the same question: how much solute is in 100 parts of the whole solution?**
