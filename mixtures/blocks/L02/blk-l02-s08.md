# How Much Can Dissolve?

<!-- STEP -->
## Solubility

Keep adding sugar to a glass of water and stirring. At some point no more will dissolve, and the extra stays at the bottom.

The maximum amount of a solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent at a given temperature is its **solubility**. We usually give it in grams per 100 g of water.

A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at that temperature is called a **saturated solution**.

This graph of solubility against temperature is a **solubility curve**. Compare the two curves: at every temperature, compound B dissolves much more than compound A.

<!-- STEP -->
## Read a curve

Drag the probe along the curve of compound B and read the solubility.

At 40 °C it is 241 g per 100 g of water. At 60 °C it is 287 g.

**A solubility without a temperature is not complete, because the number changes as the water warms.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Four real salts

These are the values in your textbook's table, from 10 °C to 80 °C. Pick each one on the chart.

- **Potassium nitrate (KNO3):** from 21 g up to 167 g, a very large rise.
- **Ammonium chloride (NH4Cl):** from 24 g up to 66 g.
- **Potassium chloride (KCl):** from 35 g up to 54 g.
- **Sodium chloride (NaCl), common salt:** from 36 g to only 37 g.

You might expect heat to help every salt in the same way, but common salt hardly changes.

<!-- STEP -->
## Gases go the other way

The solubility of a solid in a liquid generally goes up with temperature. For a gas dissolved in a liquid, it generally goes down.

Open a warm bottle of soda and a cold one. The warm one fizzes more, because warm water holds less carbon dioxide and the extra gas comes out.

**Warm water usually holds more of a solid and less of a gas.**
