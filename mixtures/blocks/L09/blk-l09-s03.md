# Seven Mixtures, Seven Reasons

<!-- STEP -->
## A table from your textbook

Your textbook gives you a table of seven mixtures. For each one, you must name a method and give the reason for choosing it.

- Mud from muddy water
- Plasma from a blood sample
- Naphthalene and sand
- Chalk powder and common salt
- Common salt and water
- Oil from water
- The pigments of a flower (a pigment is a coloured substance, here in a flower)

If a mixture cannot be separated, you must explain why. Tap a mixture in the table to open it.

<!-- STEP -->
## The reason comes first

It is natural to write the method first, by remembering which apparatus looked right. That works for a mixture you have seen, but it is only a guess for a new one.

So work the other way round. First say what is different about the two parts, and then the method follows from that. In this table you must pick the difference before you can run anything.

<!-- STEP -->
## Four mixtures

- **Mud from muddy water.** Mud is denser than water, so the heavy grains settle, but the finest grains stay up. Add alum to clump them, let the clumps settle, then pour off the clear water or filter it.
- **Plasma from a blood sample.** Blood cells are denser than plasma. A centrifuge spins the tube, and the plasma ends up on top.
- **Naphthalene and sand.** Naphthalene sublimes and sand does not. Heat the dish gently under a funnel, and the sand stays behind.
- **Oil from water.** They are immiscible, and oil is less dense than water, so they form two layers. A separating funnel lets the water out first.

<!-- STEP -->
## One mixture, three steps

Chalk powder and common salt look alike, and both are dry. You might try a filter or a flame straight away, but neither works on the dry powder. The difference is solubility, because salt dissolves in water and chalk does not.

1. **Add water.** The salt dissolves and the chalk does not.
2. **Filter.** The chalk stays on the paper and the salt water runs through.
3. **Evaporate** the salt water. The salt is left in the dish.

Evaporate before you filter, and the salt is back in the chalk. **Each step needs the step before it, so the order matters.**

<!-- STEP -->
## The last two

**Common salt and water** has two right answers, and it depends on which part you need. Evaporate if you need the salt, and the water is lost. Distil if you need the water, and the salt stays in the flask.

**The pigments of a flower** are separated by paper chromatography. Some flower colours do not dissolve in water, so use alcohol as the solvent. The colours do separate on the strip, but in tiny amounts. This method tells you what is in a mixture, and it will not fill a jar.

**All seven can be separated, and in each one a difference chose the method.**
