# Why Crystals Come Out Pure

<!-- STEP -->
## A solution with two solids in it

When a new compound is made, unwanted substances often come with it. Suppose you have 60 g of **potassium nitrate** with 5 g of **common salt** mixed in by accident.

Both dissolve in 100 g of hot water at 80 °C. The liquid is clear, so you cannot tell by looking that there are two solids in it.

Each solid has its own limit in water, shown by the line on its gauge. At 80 °C, the 60 g of potassium nitrate is far below its limit of 167 g. The 5 g of salt is far below its limit of 37 g.

<!-- STEP -->
## Cool it and watch both limits

Take the temperature down slowly. The two limits behave very differently.

- The limit for potassium nitrate falls fast: 167 g at 80 °C, 62 g at 40 °C, 32 g at 20 °C.
- The limit for salt hardly moves: it is still 36 g at 20 °C.

Just below 40 °C the falling limit reaches the 60 g in the beaker, and crystals start to form.

<!-- STEP -->
## Only one solid comes out

At 20 °C, the water can keep only 32 g of potassium nitrate dissolved, so 60 - 32 = **28 g** comes out as crystals.

The salt never reaches its limit: 5 g is far below 36 g, so all of it stays dissolved. Filter the beaker, and the crystals stay on the paper while the salt runs through with the liquid.

**The main solid passes its limit and crystallises. The small impurity stays below its limit and stays in the liquid.**

<!-- STEP -->
## When the impurity is not small

Your textbook says this works when one solid is present in small quantity and both dissolve in the same solvent. Change the salt from 5 g to 50 g to see why.

Now the salt is above its limit even at 80 °C, so 13 g of it never dissolves, and more comes out on cooling. The crystals and the salt end up on the floor together.

**Crystallization separates two solids when one is a small part of the mixture and both dissolve in the same solvent.**
