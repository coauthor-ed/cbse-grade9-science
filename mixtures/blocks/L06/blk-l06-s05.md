# Camphor Leaves Without Melting

<!-- STEP -->
## The set-up

Put a spatula of crushed camphor mixed with sand into a clean, dry china dish, and stand the dish on a wire gauze on a tripod.

Plug the nozzle of a glass funnel with cotton and turn the funnel upside down over the dish. Then put a burner under the gauze.

<!-- STEP -->
## Heat it gently

Heat the dish gently for a few minutes, and watch the dish and the inner wall of the funnel.

You might expect the camphor to melt first, as ice and wax do. It does not. No liquid appears at all, yet the camphor slowly leaves the dish, and a white solid builds up on the inner wall of the funnel. The sand stays in the dish.

**The camphor moved from the dish to the funnel without ever becoming a liquid.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Two names, one for each direction

- **Sublimation:** on heating, below its melting point, a solid changes directly into vapour without passing through the liquid state.
- **Deposition:** on cooling, vapour changes back into a solid without becoming a liquid.

The cooler glass of the funnel is where deposition happens.

<!-- STEP -->
## Pull out the cotton

What does the cotton do? Pull it out and heat a fresh dish to find out.

White solid still forms on the funnel wall, because the glass is still cool, but less of it collects. The rest of the vapour escapes through the open nozzle, and you can smell camphor in the room. The plug stops vapour from escaping, so it lets you keep nearly all of the camphor.

<!-- STEP -->
## Other solids that sublime

Naphthalene sublimes as well, so you can do the same experiment with it, and a mixture of naphthalene and salt can be separated this way. Dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide, also sublimes. It is used to store ice cream.

**The difference used here is that one part sublimes on heating and the other does not.**

Every method so far has used a difference that we could reach. Next we meet a mixture where that is much harder.
