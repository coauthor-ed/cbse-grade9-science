# A Comes Over First

<!-- STEP -->
## Heat, then cool

Heat the flask and the liquid turns to vapour. Cool the vapour and it turns back into liquid, and that liquid drips into a separate vessel.

This process is called **distillation**, and the liquid collected is called the **distillate**.

Heat the flask and watch what reaches the vessel. When the reading starts to climb again, change the vessel.

<!-- STEP -->
## Why A comes first

The first vapour comes from liquid A, because A has the lower boiling point. It turns to vapour at 60 °C, while B is still well below its boiling point.

You may have picked B, because 90 °C sounds like the bigger, hotter number. That guess is easy to make, but a higher boiling point does not mean the liquid comes first. It means more heating before the liquid boils, so that liquid comes over later.

**The liquid with the lower boiling point comes over first.**

<!-- STEP -->
## What the thermometer shows

The reading climbs to about 60 °C and then stays near 60 °C while A boils away. The heat is being used to turn A into vapour, so the temperature does not keep rising.

When nearly all of A has gone, the reading climbs again towards 90 °C. That climb tells you to change the collecting vessel, because B is about to come over.

A mixture with a flat stretch at 60 °C and another at 90 °C is giving you two separate liquids, one at a time.

<!-- STEP -->
## What distillation can do

Distillation boils a liquid, and boiling is not the same as evaporation. Evaporation happens only at the surface of a liquid, at any temperature. Boiling happens all through the liquid at its boiling point, with bubbles of vapour.

Distillation does two jobs that evaporation cannot do.

- It **gets the solvent back**. When you evaporate salt water, the water is lost to the air. When you distil it, the water is collected.
- It **separates two miscible liquids**, as long as their boiling points are far enough apart.
