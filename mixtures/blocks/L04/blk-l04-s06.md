# Fractional Distillation

<!-- STEP -->
## When the gap is too small

This part goes beyond your syllabus, but it answers a question you may be asking. What if the boiling points differ by less than 25 °C?

**Crude oil** from the Earth's crust is a mixture of many liquids and gases, and many of them boil close together. One flask cannot separate them, so a refinery uses a tall tower instead.

The process is called **fractional distillation**.

<!-- STEP -->
## A tower that is hot at the bottom and cool at the top

A furnace at the bottom heats the crude oil, and its vapour rises up the tower. The tower gets cooler with height.

As the vapour rises, it reaches a level that is cool enough for one part of it to condense. That part turns to liquid there and is drawn off. The rest stays as vapour and keeps rising.

**Each part condenses at the level that matches its boiling point.**

<!-- STEP -->
## The order of the fractions

Each part drawn off is called a **fraction**. They leave in order, and the order follows the boiling points.

| From the tower | Fraction |
|---|---|
| Top, coolest | Petroleum gas |
| | Petrol |
| | Aviation fuel (kerosene) |
| | Diesel |
| | Lubricating oil |
| Bottom, hottest | Bitumen |

Petroleum gas has the lowest boiling point, so it rises all the way to the top. Bitumen has the highest, so it stays at the bottom.

<!-- STEP -->
## The gas in your kitchen

The gas that leaves the top of the tower is filled into steel cylinders under high pressure, where it turns to liquid. This is **LPG**, liquefied petroleum gas, the domestic fuel in many Indian kitchens.

When the valve opens and the pressure drops, it turns back into gas.
