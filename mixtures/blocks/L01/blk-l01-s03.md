# B and C Show the Beam

<!-- STEP -->
## What you see

Aim the pointer at each beaker in turn.

- **A**: the beam goes in and comes out, but you see no line in the liquid.
- **B**: a bright red line crosses the liquid.
- **C**: a bright red line crosses this liquid too.

"Only B" was a reasonable guess, because B was the only beaker with grains you could see. But C shows the path as well.

**The beam found something in C that the lens could not show.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Why a beam can be seen

You see something only when light from it reaches your eye. A laser beam goes straight ahead, so from the side none of its light comes towards you.

In B and C, separate particles float in the water. When the beam hits one, some of the light is **scattered**, which means spread out in many directions. Some of that spread light reaches your eye, and you see the path.

In A the salt has dissolved. There are no particles big enough to scatter the light, so the beam stays hidden.

<!-- STEP -->
## Two words for mixtures

Mixture A is uniform throughout, and no separate particles can be found in it. That is a **homogeneous** mixture, also called a **solution**. A well-stirred sugar solution is equally sweet in the first sip and the last.

A **heterogeneous** mixture is not uniform. Its parts can be seen, or they settle, or a beam of light shows them. B is heterogeneous: you can see the chalk grains.

<!-- STEP -->
## Milk looks uniform, but it is not

Mixture C looks the same all through, and you might call it homogeneous. The beam says no, because something in C is scattering light. Your textbook also lists milk as heterogeneous.

**A mixture can look uniform and still be heterogeneous. A solution shows no beam path at all.**
