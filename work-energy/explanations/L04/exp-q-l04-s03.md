# Two Routes, One Number

<!-- STEP -->
## Do the arithmetic both ways

$U = mgh$, and the same three numbers go in each time:

$$U = 50 \times 10 \times 72.5 = 36\,250\ \mathrm{J}$$

Lift or stairs, ground floor or top floor - the expression does not notice. There is no second number to compute, and that is not a shortcut. It is the answer.

<!-- STEP -->
## Why the staircase does not add anything

Here is where the wrong answers come from, so it is worth being precise.

Climbing the stairs *is* harder work than standing in a lift. The student is lifting their own body weight the whole way, and their muscles are doing real work against gravity and against their own body. So there is extra work being done on that journey.

But work done *on the student-Earth system* is what changes the system's energy, and the muscles are **inside** that system. The student brought up the stairs chemical energy in their own body, and spent it. That energy went into heating their muscles and into the surroundings, not into raising their potential energy.

Raising the potential energy required $mgh$ of work, and $mgh$ of work raises the potential energy by $mgh$. The other joules went somewhere else entirely.

<!-- STEP -->
## The lift, for comparison

In the lift the student is not doing the lifting at all - a motor is. So the work on the student-Earth system is done by the motor, through the cable, and again it comes to $mgh$ for the same reason: the student's height has risen by $h$ and their mass has not changed.

Same gain. Different agent doing the work. Neither the path nor the agent appears in the answer.

**The route taken up a building is not a variable in U = mgh, and no amount of extra effort on the stairs can make it one.**
