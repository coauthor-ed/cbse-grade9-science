# Where the Zero Goes

<!-- STEP -->
## Concede the first half

The student's objection is right about one thing, and it should not be argued away.

$U = mgh$ measures height from a chosen level. Change the level and the number changes. Take the ground as zero and a ball on the third floor is worth $m$ times $g$ times three floors. Take the third floor as zero and it is worth nothing at all.

**That is a convention, exactly like the zero on a thermometer.** We put it at the ground because the ground is where the interesting comparisons start, not because the ground is a physically special place.

<!-- STEP -->
## Then show what survives the change

Write the energy at two heights, $h_1$ and $h_2$, measured from whatever level you like:

$$U_1 = mg h_1 \qquad U_2 = mg h_2$$

The gain from the first to the second is the difference:

$$\Delta U = mg h_2 - mg h_1 = mg\,(h_2 - h_1)$$

The zero is gone. It was never in the difference, because it cancels. And $h_2 - h_1$ is just the distance moved vertically, which is a length you can put a tape measure to.

**So the single value is a convention and the change is a measurement. That is the whole content of the objection, and it is why the expression is still useful.**

<!-- STEP -->
## The trap worth naming

There is a real effect hiding in this question, and it is not the one the student is using.

$g$ really does get smaller further from the Earth's centre. That is a genuine fact, and it is worth knowing. But over the height of one storey the change in $g$ is far too small to matter. Two hundred metres of climb barely registers.

So the two balls are not different because $g$ changed. They are different because one of them is lower.

**Move the zero and the number moves. Move the ball and the number moves too - and only one of those is something you did.**
